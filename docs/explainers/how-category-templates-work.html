Explainer

How categories, colours and icons actually work

From the bird's view down to the ant's. No prior knowledge assumed, nothing simplified into being wrong.

Flip that switch at any point while reading. Every coloured thing on this page is painted by the same mechanism the app uses, so the page is not illustrating the idea, it is doing it. When you reach the section on themes, come back up here and watch what changes and what does not.

Altitude 1 of 6 · the bird

The whole thing in five sentences

Somewhere there is a shared list of the categories the app offers a new user, and each entry on it names a colour and an icon. When you finish signing up, the app does not point your account at that shared list. It copies the entries you picked into a private database that belongs to you alone. From then on your categories are yours: renaming one changes nothing for anybody else, and an administrator editing the shared list changes nothing for you. The only things that ever travel from that shared list into your account are four short pieces of text per category, and two of them are the interesting ones, because the colour is stored as a word rather than as a colour.

Altitude 2 of 6

Why a database, when a list in the code worked fine

Until recently that list of categories was written into the program itself, the way a printed menu is part of the sign above the counter. Changing "Dining out" to "Eating out" meant a developer editing a file, a review, and a release. That is an absurd amount of ceremony for changing a word, and it means the only people who can adjust what the app offers are the people who can rebuild it.

Moving the list into a database changes who holds the pen. A database row is just data, and data can be edited by an administrator through a screen, taking effect immediately, with no release. That is the entire motivation. Everything else in this document is a consequence of that one decision, and most of the interesting parts are consequences nobody would guess.

What became editable

  • Which categories a new user is offered
  • What each is called and described as
  • Which colour and icon each one carries
  • The order they appear in
  • Whether a colour or icon is offered at all

What stayed a release

  • Inventing an eighteenth colour
  • Adding a fourteenth icon
  • Changing what a colour word means

That split looks arbitrary right now. Two sections from here it will look inevitable.

Altitude 3 of 6

The menu and the plate

The single most important thing to hold onto is that your account copies from the shared list rather than referring to it. A restaurant menu says "risotto". You order it, and a plate of risotto arrives. The plate is now yours. If the kitchen renames the dish that afternoon, nothing happens to your plate. If you push the peas to one side, the menu is unaffected. The plate and the menu stopped being connected the moment the food was served.

Software very often does the opposite, storing a pointer back to the original so that everything stays in step. That would have been the wrong choice here, and it is worth seeing why concretely.

If something happensCopying (what the app does)Referring (the alternative)
You rename your "Groceries" to "Food" Your row changes. Nobody else is affected. You would be editing everybody's category, or be forbidden from editing at all.
An admin renames the template to "Food shopping" Nothing happens to you. Only future sign-ups see it. Your category silently renames itself overnight.
An admin deletes a template Nothing happens to you. Your category still exists. Your category vanishes, and so does the meaning of every transaction in it.
You delete a category you no longer use Your row is removed and its transactions move to "Uncategorized". You would be deleting an entry from a shared catalogue.

The copy carries four things: the name, the colour word, the icon name, and the description, which becomes the note on your own category. That is all. Notice there is no leftover thread tying the two together, which is exactly the property that makes the privacy claim simple: an administrator editing templates has no route into anybody's account, not because they are forbidden, but because there is nothing to travel along.

Altitude 4 of 6 · the important one

A colour is stored as a word, not as a colour

Here is the part that surprises people. Your "Groceries" category does not store green. It stores the word success. There is no green anywhere in either database. There is no green anywhere in the app's stored data at all.

success is not a colour, it is a role. It means roughly "the positive one". Alongside it are sixteen other role names: primary is "the main one", warning is "the cautionary one", accent is "the decorative one", and so on. Think of a theatre programme listing the parts rather than the actors: Hamlet, Ophelia, the gravedigger. The programme does not tell you who is playing them tonight.

The theme is the casting. It is a single table that says, for tonight's performance, which actual colour plays each role. The app ships two of them, a light theme and a dark theme, and your operating system picks which is on. For some roles the two performances genuinely have different actors. primary is one of them, and it is the reason a stored colour could never have worked:

Most roles keep the same colour. It is the paper that changes.

This is the part almost everybody gets wrong first, including the first draft of this page. You would expect a dark theme to darken everything. It does not. Measured against the app's own installed theme:

RoleLightDark
primary#422ad5#605dffdifferent
success#00d390#00d390identical
secondary#f43098#f43098identical
warning#fcb700#fcb700identical
base-100 (the card behind everything)#ffffff#1d232adifferent

So switching to dark mostly does not repaint the categories at all. It repaints the surface they sit on, from white to near-black. The green never moved; the paper under it did.

That single fact explains the numbers in the palette file next door , which otherwise look bizarre. The green scores 1.959 against the light card and 8.084 against the dark one, a fourfold difference, for a colour that is byte-for-byte the same in both. A mid-bright green on white is nearly invisible; the identical green on near-black is vivid. Contrast is a relationship between two colours, so changing either end changes the answer, and a theme mostly changes the end nobody was looking at.

It is also why a colour cannot be judged from a swatch on its own, and why both reference files draw every colour on the app's real card rather than on a white page.

Why storing the colour itself would have been broken

Not merely roundabout. Actually incorrect. The role primary is a deep indigo in the light theme and a brighter violet in the dark one. Those are two different colours. If your "Entertainment" category stored one of them, it would be wrong every time you were in the other theme, and there is no third colour that could be right in both. The word is the only thing that can be stored that stays true in every theme, because the word is the question and the theme is the answer.

So how does flipping the theme actually reach the pixel?

This is worth following precisely, because it explains why the theme switch is instant and why the database is never consulted when it happens.

The word travels out of your database, through the server, into the web page, and lands in the page as a label attached to a box: this box is a success box. It is still a word. No colour has been decided yet.

Separately, the browser holds the theme, which is a short list of role-to-colour pairs attached to the page as a whole. When the browser paints the box, it reads the label, looks the role up in whichever list is currently active, and uses what it finds. When the theme changes, that list is swapped, the browser repaints, and every box wearing a role label lands on a new colour in the same instant.

Two consequences fall out of that split, and both are worth stating plainly. Nothing is re-fetched when you change theme. No request goes to the server, no database is opened, nothing is recalculated. The colour was never sent in the first place, only the role name was, so there is nothing to send again. And no part of the app has to be told about a new theme. The category code does not know how many themes exist. It says "success" and stops.

Altitude 5 of 6

Why the admin gets seventeen colours and not a colour picker

This is the constraint the whole design bends around, and it comes from an unglamorous place: the app's styling is assembled before the app runs, by a tool that reads the program's text and works out which visual instructions could possibly be needed. It then produces exactly those and nothing else, which is why the app is small and fast.

That tool runs at build time. The database does not exist yet. So a colour that is only named in a database row is a colour that tool never saw, and the instruction for painting it is never produced. The failure is quiet in the worst way: nothing errors, no warning appears, the box simply comes out transparent.

The fix is to decide the whole set of possible colours in advance and write all seventeen out longhand in the program, so the tool sees every one. The database then never invents a colour, it only chooses from that set. An administrator gets to say which of the seventeen a category uses, what each is called in the picker ("Emerald", "Pine", "Slate"), what order they appear in and whether one is offered at all. Adding an eighteenth is a release, because a genuinely new colour has to be seen by that tool, and the only way to be seen by it is to be in the code.

Seventeen rather than thirteen because the list is not the category list. Twelve starter categories plus the "Uncategorized" fallback account for thirteen; the remaining four exist so that the first category you invent for yourself is not forced to duplicate a colour already in use. One of the seventeen, error-content, is deliberately switched off in the picker: it is very nearly the same brightness as the dark background, so a category wearing it would be invisible half the time. It stays on the permitted list so that a category which somehow already has it still draws, and off the offered list so nobody new can choose it. The sibling file category-color-palette-preview.html shows all seventeen side by side with their measurements.

The icons work the same way, for a different reason

An icon in this app is not a picture file. It is a short set of drawing instructions, an SVG: move here, curve there, close the shape. That matters for two reasons. It stays perfectly sharp at any size, and, crucially, it can be told to draw itself in whatever colour its surroundings are using.

Your category stores the icon as a name, exactly as it stores the colour as a word: shopping-basket. The drawing instructions live in a library of about sixteen hundred glyphs. Fetching one by name at the moment it is needed would mean shipping all sixteen hundred to every visitor, because the app cannot know in advance which one a database row will ask for. So the app keeps a hand-written list of the sixty-four it actually offers, pairing each name with its drawing instructions, and ships exactly those sixty-four. Same shape of answer as the colours, same underlying cause: the app has to know the full set of possibilities before it runs.

The last step is the neat one. The glyph is not given a colour of its own. It is told to draw itself in "whatever the current text colour is", and each of the seventeen roles comes paired with a partner role meant for whatever sits on top of it. So a success tile sets its text colour to success-content, and the basket picks that up automatically. The result is a glyph that is legible on every one of the seventeen backgrounds without anybody choosing seventeen glyph colours, and that follows the theme along with everything else, because both halves of the pair are roles rather than colours.

The same colour, painted four different ways

One category's colour shows up in four places in the app, and although they look like variations on one thing, the app genuinely needs three different recipes for them. This is the sort of detail that seems pedantic until it causes a bug, which it has.

MarkWhereRecipeWhy it differs
the transactions list background and its partner for the glyph something is drawn on top, so the top needs a colour too
the little dot beside a category name background only nothing on top, so the partner colour is pointless here
the sign-up chips background only, and it must be this dot draws a soft shadow from the text colour, so giving it one turns the shadow into a smudge
the dashboard donut the colour as a value, not as a label drawings take a colour directly and reject a label, silently painting nothing

All four are live above, so they all move together when you flip the theme. That is the point of the whole arrangement: four different recipes, one stored word, and no chance of one of them ending up a different green from the others.

Altitude 6 of 6 · the ant

One category's entire life, step by step

Everything above, now as a single trace, with the real file names in case you ever want to go and look.

  1. 1. Before anybody signs up

    The shared database is created empty and immediately filled with its starting contents: seventeen colours with their picker labels, sixty-four icons, twelve categories. This happens in the program rather than by hand, once, and it checks first whether anything is already there, so that a restart cannot resurrect a category an administrator deliberately deleted. backend/src/database/central/template-seed.ts

  2. 2. You reach step two of sign-up

    The page asks the shared database for the category list. This is the one request in the app that needs no account, because you do not have one yet. If it fails, you get an empty list rather than an error page, on the reasoning that losing your starter categories is a far smaller harm than being unable to sign up. GET /api/templates/categories

  3. 3. You tick some chips and submit

    What is sent is the identifiers of the templates you ticked, not their names. Nothing is created yet: your choices sit in your browser and a pending record, and an email goes out. Nothing about you is trusted until you prove the address is yours.

  4. 4. You click the link in the email

    This is the moment the account becomes real. A database is created for you alone, and each template you picked is copied into it: name, colour word, icon name, and the description, which becomes your note. A thirteenth category, "Uncategorized", is added for everybody. That one is deliberately not a template, because its name is a rule of the system rather than something an admin may rename. backend/src/database/user/starter-categories.ts

  5. 5. You open the transactions page

    Your categories are read from your own database. A row comes back saying, in effect, "Groceries, success, shopping-basket". Still no colour, still no picture.

  6. 6. The page turns those two words into a tile

    Two hand-written lists do the translation: one turns success into the pair of labels that paint a background and its glyph colour, the other turns shopping-basket into the actual drawing instructions. Neither can be skipped, and both are checked by the compiler against the list the server publishes, so a colour the server can send but the page cannot paint stops the build. frontend/src/components/ui/categoryColour.ts

  7. 7. Your browser paints it

    The role labels are resolved against whichever theme is active, and the tile appears. The glyph, told only to use "the current text colour", comes out in the partner colour and is legible.

  8. 8. Night falls and your operating system switches to dark

    The theme in force changes. Every role resolves to its dark-theme colour and the page repaints. No request is made, nothing is re-read, no database is touched, and not one line of category code runs. The word in your database is unchanged and was always the only thing stored.

What keeps it honest, and two times it did not

Two things about this design are load-bearing and easy to lose, and both have already been broken once, which is the best possible argument for writing them down.

The accounts that were created before the change

Category rows are written once, at sign-up, and nothing rewrites them. When colours stopped being colours and became words, every account created earlier still held actual colour values, which the new translation list did not recognise. Every category on every screen for those users fell back to a plain grey tile with no glyph. Two builds, a lint run and six hundred tests were green throughout, because every test builds its own fresh data. It was found by opening the app. The repair is a one-off conversion that runs the first time each account is touched, decides what to do by looking at the data itself rather than at a marker, and cannot half apply.

"It is a theme colour, so it must be visible"

The "Uncategorized" category was briefly given a role whose colour, in the dark theme, is almost exactly as bright as the background it sits on. The reasoning written down at the time was that a theme-aware colour is safe by definition. It is not. Roles come in pairs at opposite ends of the brightness range, which is exactly what makes each one readable on its partner and exactly what makes many of them invisible on the page. The sibling palette file exists so this is settled by looking rather than by reasoning, and the guard is written up in the frontend's own notes under Changing or adding a theme.

The moral of both is the same and worth carrying past this app. Everything described here is checked by a machine except the one thing that matters most to a person: whether the result is actually legible. No build, test or automated check in this project reads a colour. That job belongs to a human with these three files open.
